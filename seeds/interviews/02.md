I've been teaching for eleven years now, the last six in self-contained autism support classrooms across three districts. I'll share what I keep telling new families because it doesn't get said often enough.

First: the school placement decision is usually the most consequential one parents make in the first few years post-diagnosis. People obsess over therapy hours and miss this. I've watched kids stagnate in the wrong setting and bloom in the right one. The setting matters more than the curriculum brand.

Northpoint Academy is the one our district has the most experience placing kids into. It's a private day school, and what I tell parents is: it's a strong fit for kids whose primary support need is language and structure, less so for kids whose primary need is regulation. They have great speech integration but their behavioral approach is fairly traditional and doesn't accommodate sensory-driven dysregulation as gracefully. Class sizes around 8.

Beacon Hill Preschool — I refer 4-year-olds there constantly. The inclusion model with embedded special ed staff is the gold standard for that age. The kids who go there for two years before kindergarten almost always do well in mainstream-with-supports later, in my experience.

For older kids who have significant sensory needs, I lean on what the Park 2018 sensory paper called the "functional sensory profile" framework. The idea is to map a kid's sensory thresholds onto the environments they're being asked to function in and use that map to redesign IEP supports. It's not magic, but it's the most actionable framework I've come across. I'd love a school that built its whole curriculum around it, but I haven't found one.

A newer option I've been watching: Cedarbrook School in Westchester opened about three years ago, very small, and they're explicitly using a sensory-first curriculum design. I've placed two kids there and the early results are encouraging but it's too early to call.

The other thing I want to flag: schools are wildly variable on how they handle the IEP-to-real-classroom translation. The IEP can say all the right things and the classroom can still be a disaster. The single best predictor I've found is whether the lead teacher meets weekly with related-service providers. Weekly. Not monthly. If they meet monthly, the kid is operating on stale information.

One question I keep getting from parents that I don't have a great answer for: how do you plan a sensory diet that survives the school day, when the school isn't trained to deliver it? I refer out to private OT, but the handoff is always awkward.
