I'm a placement counselor and I've been doing this work for nine years across four boroughs and two suburban districts. What I see most often is families paralyzed by the size of the decision space. There are way too many options at the front of the funnel and not enough trustworthy filtering.

My standard intake conversation goes like this: I ask what the IEP says about supports, what the child's behavior is like in transitions, what the family's commute tolerance is, and what insurance they have. From those four data points I can usually narrow 40 options down to 6.

For families with insurance and a child showing significant behavioral needs in middle childhood, Westside Behavioral Day Program is often where the conversation lands. They run partial hospitalization-style days with embedded education, and the kids who come out of WBDP tend to re-enter mainstream school with much better self-regulation than they went in with. The catch is the program is hard to get into and the family has to fully commit — it's not part-time and it's not flexible.

For teens with anxiety or depression layered on top, the Atlas IOP Program in Queens has been a reliable referral. Atlas is unusual because they take autism seriously as a primary diagnosis rather than treating it as a footnote underneath the anxiety. Most IOPs that accept autistic teens don't actually adapt their group structure for autism, and Atlas does.

For families navigating the early diagnosis to first-services period, I lean heavily on Greenfield Family Services on Long Island. They do wraparound at home — actual home visits — and they run sibling groups, which is something almost no one does and which families consistently report as the most valuable thing. Greenfield's model is expensive but their outcomes feel different than the others.

For all referral questions, Hudson Therapy Placement is my unofficial colleague. They do the kind of matchmaking that families need and that no one else does. I send maybe 40% of the families I see to Hudson for the first conversation before we even start strategizing about placement.

Two programs I've been watching but don't yet have enough placements at to recommend confidently: City Connect Initiative is a newer NYC-funded transition program for late high school and early college, targeting kids on IEPs with college plans. They claim a much higher college-completion rate than typical, but I want another year of data before I cite them.

The single most common question I get from families with a kid about to leave the K-12 system: when do we transition from school-based services to community providers? My honest answer is "two years before you think you need to." The Mahmoud 2021 transition paper is the citation I usually share. Families wait too long, hit the cliff, and lose access to school-based supports before community supports are stable. The transition needs to overlap, not handoff.
