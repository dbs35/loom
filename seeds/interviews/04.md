I was diagnosed at 28. I'm 33 now. I want to share what I've learned because there's almost nothing written for adults who get diagnosed late.

First, on the diagnostic process. Getting an adult autism diagnosis in the US is genuinely difficult unless you can pay out of pocket — most insurance won't cover it, most evaluators who do adults don't take insurance, and many evaluators who take insurance don't have meaningful adult training. I paid $2,800 cash for mine. The places I called that took insurance had 9-month waitlists and required a referral. Where adults can get diagnosed affordably is one of the most-asked questions in every late-diagnosed adult group I'm part of, and the honest answer is "you basically can't, unless you're willing to wait a year or move to a state with better Medicaid coverage."

Second, on what changed for me after diagnosis. Reading the Reyes 2020 paper on autistic burnout as a conceptual framework was the single most useful thing I've done. It named a thing I'd been experiencing for ten years and didn't have language for. I'd had three episodes I'd previously labeled as depression — which they weren't, exactly — and the burnout framework explained the relationship between high-masking work, social demands, and what I'd been calling crashes. If I'd had that vocabulary at 22 I'd have made different choices about graduate school.

Third, on the transition piece. The Mahmoud 2021 paper on transition outcomes is essential reading for any young adult on the spectrum or their family. The paper's most important finding, in my reading, is that the kids who do best post-21 aren't necessarily the highest-functioning by traditional measures — they're the ones whose support structures continued past the school cliff. Summit Bridges, where my younger brother spent two years post-high school, is one of the rare programs that operates in the 18-26 range and treats the transition as a multi-year process. He's now living semi-independently with a roommate, working part-time, and the structure they built around him during those years is exactly what Mahmoud's paper says works.

Fourth, on community. I'm involved with the Autistic Self Advocacy Network NYC Chapter, which is autistic-led and runs monthly peer groups in person. ASAN NYC is small but growing. The local model — autistic-led, not "for autistics" — has been the single most healing thing in my adult life. There's a thing that happens in a room full of autistic people that doesn't happen in any other space.

A question I want to flag for other people figuring this out: how do you actually find autistic-led peer support? This is genuinely hard. Most autism organizations are parent-led, which is fine but a different kind of community. Peer-led groups exist but they aren't well-indexed and they don't market themselves.
